\section{Two Realisations of Spec-Driven Planning}
\label{sec:discussions-sdd}

Both frameworks plan and create specifications before they build, and both explore the codebase and dispatch subagents for the heavier research. They differ in who makes the planning decisions. Spec-kit has the agent resolve the open questions itself, making informed guesses based on the model's training data. It fills a fixed set of template documents from what it finds and knows, and it turns to the user only through an optional step that offers recommended choices to confirm. Its planning is therefore sensitive to the model's capability and to how the autonomous exploration will play out. APM keeps the user as the authority. Its Planner goes through a series of question rounds with the user and brings back what it understood for approval before any document is written. It is directed to ask rather than assume, and it produces a few short planning documents that generalise across project types. The task brief the participants worked from was already detailed, yet under it APM still asked many targeted questions grounded in its research, while Spec-kit, with the same brief, asked little. Both gate their planning and ask the user for approval. APM's gates are points where the user approves each planning document, while Spec-kit's are the agent's own validation checks and a holistic presentation to the user at the end.

The planning turns differed in kind accordingly, as the planning buckets of Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition} report. Spec-kit, planning mostly on its own, turned to the user to confirm recommended choices and approve them rather than to answer open questions. APM instead brought the user's decisions into the planning, requiring more substantive interactions.

That difference in authority does not reach the size of the planning phase, which carries no firm difference in cost, duration, or turn-prompt count, as the planning rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-paired-by-scope}, Table~\ref{tab:proc-time-paired-by-scope}, and Table~\ref{tab:proc-turns-paired} report. What differs is where the planning work took place, which Table~\ref{tab:disc-planning-behaviour-paired} details. Under Spec-kit the main agent did that work itself, issuing more requests to the model in eleven of the twelve pairs and making more tool calls in the same eleven. Under APM part of it was offloaded to subagents, which made more tool calls than Spec-kit's in ten of the twelve pairs.

Both frameworks had sessions that fell well short. Five across the cohort scored below fifty, two under APM and three under Spec-kit, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-sessions} details, so the shortfall was not one framework's alone. They did not all fail in the same way. The re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox} lifts a fault that occurred under both frameworks, leaving two Spec-kit sessions below fifty.

In Spec-kit's largely autonomous planning, a step that settles an open question without the user can do so wrongly, and the build then rests on that decision. APM puts its open questions to the user in the Planner's question rounds and holds each planning document at a review gate. A wrong assumption therefore meets the user before the build inherits it. The two remaining low-score sessions align with that difference in planning authority, but they do not establish that it produced them.

\section{Coordinating the Implementation}
\label{sec:discussions-implementation}

Both frameworks build against their planning documents and revise them as findings require. Spec-kit carries the implementation out in the same agent that also planned. In APM, a Manager and its Workers carry it out across several agents and coordinate through the user. The several agents shape what the implementation costs, and that coordination shapes what it demands of the user.

\subsection{The Cost of Several Agents}
\label{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics}

Under per-token pricing, each of APM's added conversations built its own cache, produced its own output, and accumulated its own context. APM's spending therefore exceeded Spec-kit's in the output and cache-write categories, as the cohort rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-category} detail. The rate card in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card} lists output and one-hour cache writes as the two most expensive of the five token categories, which puts most of APM's excess on the costliest tokens. APM's higher overall cost rests on this implementation excess alone, since the planning phase carries no firm cost difference, as the phase rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-paired-by-scope} report.

The two model groups did not bear this cost in the same way. The difference lies in the size of each model's context window and how the platform handles a conversation that reaches its limit. As Section~\ref{sec:background-context-windows-and-context-decay} describes, each call costs more than the last as the context grows, and a compaction resets it to a low starting point. In Claude Code, compactions happen automatically as a conversation nears its limit, and the user may also trigger one manually. APM has its own form of this reset, the Handoff described in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework-design}, which in cost terms works the same way as a compaction.\footnote{Forthcoming. Framework Design will define APM's design principles, roles, phases, Message Bus, memory, Handoff, and distribution.} As Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} reports, such resets happened in only one of the two model groups.

The Sonnet 4.6 model used in our study had a two-hundred-thousand-token context window. Conversations often grew near its ceiling and reset, either auto-compacted by the platform or compacted manually by the participant. A Sonnet conversation that compacted therefore used more than one context window, clearing its cache and building its context anew. Opus 4.7, by contrast, had a one-million-token context window, large enough to hold the entire task from start to finish.

Figures~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-opus} and~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-sonnet} plot the peak each context window reached within the two model groups. A conversation that compacted twice, for example, resolves to three peaks, one for each context window it used. The highest markers are Spec-kit conversations that grew long without a reset. APM's conversations generally sit below them, most in a dense low cluster, since dividing the work leaves each agent with less context to accumulate.

A few APM markers nonetheless reach the high ranges, one even passing the Spec-kit conversation it is paired with. These are all APM Workers. They accumulate the most context of any agent role in an APM session, and account for the largest part of what the session cost, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-session-levels-by-role} details.

Under Opus no conversation reset, so each used only one context window, growing from the start of the task to its end. Each call is charged for the whole context before it, and APM's conversations together held more accumulated context than Spec-kit's one. All three paired summaries were positive for output and both cache-write categories, as the Opus rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-category} report. Input was negligible, while the cache-read summaries were positive despite one negative pair. The cache-write differences were positive in every pair, while output and cache-read differences were positive in five of six. The wide gaps of Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-opus} show the accumulation behind those differences, with Spec-kit holding a few lone high markers and APM a spread that reaches toward them.

Under Sonnet, both frameworks faced the tighter window's pressure. Spec-kit's conversation reached the limit and compacted in five of its six sessions, more than once in three. Every APM session had one reset as well, always in the Worker assigned the C part of the task, the heaviest work in the build. Four auto-compacted, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} reports, and the other two reset through a Worker Handoff instead, which the table's compaction tally does not capture.

In cost terms a Spec-kit conversation that compacted behaved as a succession of agents starting over each time, building cache and accumulating context again in a fresh context window. Under Sonnet that pattern brought Spec-kit's cost closer to APM's. This shows in the cache reading. Spec-kit read more from the cache than APM, the reverse of the Opus case, as the Sonnet cache-read row of Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-category} reports. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-sonnet} shows why. Spec-kit drove one conversation up to the compaction ceiling and back, paying for a large context on every request as it climbed. APM held most of its conversations well below the ceiling, where each call had to reread less.

A platform issue we observed during the study complicates the Sonnet reading. A model cannot serve a request past its context window, and Claude Code auto-compacts near the limit to prevent reaching it. In four Spec-kit sessions the conversation passed the auto-compaction ceiling without resetting and went on serving requests past it, which shows in the highest points of Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-sonnet}. Claude Code reported Sonnet and its two-hundred-thousand-token window throughout, and eventually its context window consumption reading went past full. These sessions appear to have been served by a larger variant the platform never surfaced. The accumulation shows only in Spec-kit, since no APM session passed the window, and it plausibly lifted Spec-kit's Sonnet cache reads.

The issue does not overturn the cost reading, but what it adds is not incidental. The extra context is charged as cache reading, which is the cheapest category per token and at the same time the largest part of what a session cost under either framework, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-composition-by-model} reports. Table~\ref{tab:disc-above-ceiling-cost} gives the cost of what these four conversations read from cache above the auto-compaction ceiling, from \$0.33 to \$1.96. \subsection{The Demands on the User}
\label{sec:discussions-demands}

APM routes planning and implementation through the user, while Spec-kit leaves more of both to its agent. Spec-kit's planning autonomy extends into implementation as it works through the task list largely on its own.

That difference appears in the user's input. Table~\ref{tab:proc-turns-levels} reports far more turn prompts under APM than under Spec-kit. In implementation, Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition} shows that most of APM's prompts are protocol. These protocol prompts are the per-cycle relay APM's design places in the user's hands, each being a dispatch out or a report back rather than an instruction. Standard prompts are few under both frameworks. Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition-by-role} in Appendix~\ref{app:results-process-breakdowns} reports that APM's few standard prompts reach the Manager rather than the Workers. Together, the turn-prompt and role data show that participants passed through APM's coordination points. They demonstrate the mechanism operating, though prompt counts alone cannot tell how much control participants exercised through it.

Spec-kit asks much less. Its few turns are the protocol command that launches the implementation and an occasional short or standard follow-up, too few to read as a settled composition. The agent proceeds largely on its own, and the user has little to do until the build is done.

Because APM has several agents carry out its implementation where Spec-kit has one, it has a naturally larger model request count. APM issued more requests than Spec-kit in every one of the twelve pairs, and its main agents made more tool calls in all twelve as well, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-implementation-behaviour-paired} reports. Both reverse the planning phase, where Spec-kit's main agent led on the two measures. Each agent additionally produces its own output, which is part of why APM's implementation cost the more of the two, as Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics} explains.

\section{Behind the Functional Score}
\label{sec:discussions-blackbox}

The functional comparison uses the grader's total scores, though a grader like ours can miss sound work. Those scores can also be read alongside what each framework cost.

\subsection{Grading with a Black Box}
\label{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}

The grader judges a submission only by the behaviour of the running program, never its source, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures-functional} describes. A single fault that blocks the program's responses can therefore be recorded as near-total failure, even where the rest of the implementation may be sound.

The C part of the task required every participant to implement a JSON parser and route each MCP request to its requested operation. MCP carries requests as JSON-RPC objects\footnote{MCP transport specification available at: \url{https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/transports}}, and the JSON standard treats whitespace between fields as insignificant.\footnote{RFC 8259, Section 2 (\url{https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8259}).} Three Sonnet implementations had a particular defect. Their parsers accepted a compact request such as \texttt{\{"id":1\}}, but not the ordinary spaced form \texttt{\{"id": 1\}} that the grader sends. A conformant parser should accept both forms.

Each scored 34.0 points, with the Python part complete and almost all of the C part lost. The spacing defect meant that our grader could not exercise the affected C programs' behaviour because its ordinary JSON requests elicited no response. When re-graded with compact requests, the three sessions received diagnostic scores from 93.9 to 100.0. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-grading-compact-comparison} plots the full Sonnet group, and Table~\ref{tab:disc-grading-compact-sessions} gives the three changed scores.

The re-grade recovers three of the five sessions below fifty, both APM sessions and one Spec-kit session. The two scores it does not lift remain indeterminate. They may conceal sound work beyond the re-grade's reach, or they may be genuine shortfalls. The three recovered sessions belong to two participant pairs. In one, both framework sessions carried the defect, so compact input moves the APM-minus-Spec-kit difference from 0.0 to $+5.0$ points. In the other, only the APM session was affected, moving the difference from $+4.4$ to $+64.3$ points. The diagnostic pairs would make the cohort score pattern more favourable to APM, but they do not replace the standard total-score summaries.

\subsection{Cost versus Score}
\label{sec:discussions-cost-score}

Across the cohort, APM cost more, while its total-score median, Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and mean were positive. The cohort score interval includes zero, so the paired score comparison carries no firm difference, as Sections~\ref{sec:results-economic} and~\ref{sec:results-functional} report. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-cost-score-comparison} places the paired values together. Most participants had an APM cost premium with a score gap at or near zero. Under Opus, the larger cost premium came with both sessions in most pairs close to the score ceiling. Under Sonnet, costs were close and APM's paired score summaries were more positive.

The same plot shows three participant pairs that depart from that pattern. In each, APM scored clearly higher while costing about the same or less. These are individual observations, not a cohort result.

What APM's added cost accompanied was its more structured workflow, which the total score does not read directly. As it turned out, the task was bounded and self-contained. With either model used in this study and without either framework guiding it, Claude Code could plausibly work through the task largely on its own while dispatching subagents where needed. The study did not test that counterfactual, so it leaves open whether that underlying coding agent would achieve total scores comparable to the framework-guided sessions, or how our grader would read its work. APM's heavier structure therefore had little measured total-score gap to close, while whether that structure earns its cost lies beyond what the total score can show.

\section{Perceived Versus Measured Behaviour}
\label{sec:discussions-perception}

Section~\ref{sec:results-perception} reports each questionnaire rating. We compare those ratings with recorded behaviour to identify agreement, divergence, and what the recorded behaviour alone cannot show. The control participants experienced over the build is one such value.

Effectiveness and confidence favour APM far more strongly than the total-score comparison does. No participant rated Spec-kit higher on either, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports. The preference appears genuine. Yet the paired total-score comparison carries no firm difference across the cohort, so higher scores alone do not explain it. The accounts that follow point instead to participants' role in the process and the control they retained over the build.

One participant, who used APM in their second session, wrote that they were ``much more confident in APMs output'' despite ``not having manually checked either of the code output''. They named the framework being ``strict as to enforcing the PRD when speckit was more lenient'' as the reason. Their confidence reads as trust in a specification the participant helped settle during planning. It is not a judgement of code they verified, and it echoes the reading Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd} already builds from the score point of view. The Spec-kit accounts name the corresponding lack of control directly. One participant wrote that they ``felt lack of control when it came to the deliverables and how they were produced''. Another found it odd that the framework did not operate interactively, letting the agent take its own decisions with the user left out entirely.

Effort is the one item where APM is behind. It was the more effortful framework for a slight majority of participants, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports. That effort does not align with where APM adds turns. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-demands} reads APM's extra implementation turns as the intended user-mediated coordination. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd} reads planning turns the other way. The turn-prompt count there sits level between the frameworks, but APM's prompts are the more substantive ones, since its Planner brings real open questions to the user while gathering context. One participant described planning as the ``most demanding thing'' because it involved ``questions about the whole plan in advance''. They then wrote that the Workers executed the task ``easily without any assistance from me'', apart from authorising terminal commands and checking that it aligned with the task.

Steering confirms the same reading. Delivered steers stayed near zero under both frameworks, as Section~\ref{sec:results-process-interaction} reports. Frustration also points the same way. APM was still rated the less frustrating framework, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports, despite the added time and turns, so the added burden did not compound into felt friction.

Mental demand and the effort spent checking the AI's output have no measured counterpart. That absence is a deliberate finding. Some of the cognitive work that AI-assisted development demands leaves almost no trace in the instrument set. Self-reporting attempts to fill that gap. One participant's account of a Spec-kit session is the clearest instance. They wrote, ``I didn't have a way to validate the results of the session despite taking the agents word which run tests with the mock instance''. They added, ``I didn't do my due diligence in reviewing the code output''. That session's total score is the lowest in the cohort, so here the participant's own doubt and the measured outcome point the same way.

\section{APM's Value Across the Findings}
\label{sec:discussions-structure-tradeoff}

Across the cohort, APM performed on par with GitHub's Spec-kit on total score. Its median, Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and mean all favoured APM, although the cohort interval includes zero. This positive pattern concentrated under Sonnet, while both frameworks' Opus scores remained near the ceiling. With six pairs in each model group, the split describes where the cohort pattern appeared rather than establishing a model-specific effect.

APM achieved that functional outcome with longer sessions in every pair and a higher cost across the cohort. The task appeared bounded and self-contained, but still technically demanding; however, APM's structure may have supplied more coordination than work of this scale required. Larger codebases, harder tasks, and longer development periods would give its separation of planning, implementation, and context more room to prove useful, though this study could not test that setting.

The cost trade-off itself changed with the model. Under Opus, APM's several conversations accumulated more context at the model's higher rate and larger context window, while both frameworks scored close to full marks. Under Sonnet, APM's paired score summaries were more positive and the two frameworks cost about the same. Spec-kit's single conversation repeatedly reached the smaller context window limit and reset, while APM kept most conversations smaller. This pattern suggests that APM's file-based division may carry less economic overhead with models that have smaller context windows.

The process and perception findings give that structure value beyond the total score. The recorded interaction shows that participants remained involved in APM's planning and implementation. Ratings and accounts favour APM on effectiveness and confidence, while participants describe a stronger sense of control. For users who want that role in AI-assisted development, APM preserves their involvement in the work and their authority over the software produced.

\section{Learning Effects}
\label{sec:discussions-learning-effects}

The within-subjects design has each participant carry out the same task twice, with the environment reset between the two sessions so that no code, workspace state, or assistant context passes from the first into the second. Anything a participant took from their first attempt could then lift their second, and left unaccounted it would be indistinguishable from an effect of the framework used. Reading the cohort for such an effect holds to the same reproducible standard as the rest of the chapter, and sets transcript reading aside.

That standard fixes what evidence the question can draw on. The chapter measures each session along four dimensions, and the score is not the only one that could in principle bear on learning. How a participant interacted with the framework and its agents, the interaction measures of Section~\ref{sec:results-process-interaction}, might shift as the task grew familiar. The obstacle is that the comparison here is within participant, between two sessions that used different frameworks. The two demand such different interaction that the difference between a participant's two sessions is already large from the change of framework alone. A learning-driven change would mesh into that framework difference rather than standing apart. The cost a session incurred and the time it took stand the same way, each shaped first by the framework. The score is different in kind, being the task's graded outcome on one rubric rather than framework-shaped behaviour, so a within-participant difference is a common measure a practiced participant should raise. We therefore read the score alone to investigate learning effects.

One limit still bounds what the score can show of a learning effect: the rubric's hundred-point ceiling. Most sessions scored near it, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-levels} reports. Table~\ref{tab:func-score-sessions} details the nine of twelve participants who already scored above ninety in their first session. A practiced second session had almost no room to register a gain, and only three participants entered their second with substantial ground to recover.

The individual changes have no common direction. Four participants scored higher in their second session, four scored lower, and four had the same score, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-learning-by-participant} sets out. Nine changes lay between a two-point loss and a six-point gain. Of the three larger movements, P002 gained 53 points while P001 and P012 lost 23.4 and 12.3 points. These movements all favour APM regardless of its position, so they cannot separate a practice effect from the framework or from session-to-session variation.

P010 has a different reading under the diagnostic re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}. The first-session diagnostic score remains $29.6$ while the second rises to $93.9$. The two sessions differ by $64.3$ points on the diagnostic score and by only $4.4$ on the total. The second session's diagnostic score demonstrates that this re-grade recovers a strong implementation. Since the re-grade reaches only one fault class, however, the first session's unchanged diagnostic score cannot establish that the first implementation was unsound. The diagnostic difference remains unable to separate learning from the change of framework.

The order split supports the same cautious reading. Table~\ref{tab:func-score-paired-by-order} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-functional-breakdowns} separates the paired total-score difference by which framework each participant met first, and both confidence intervals span zero. Counterbalancing distributed first exposure across the frameworks, although the realised seven-to-five allocation was not perfectly even.

The total scores therefore offer no clear cohort-wide learning direction. The individual changes are mixed, and the score ceiling limits what improvement could appear. The slightly uneven order allocation is a secondary qualification rather than the source of that uncertainty. These results cannot confirm or measure a learning effect for any one participant.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussions-threats}

Each threat below states what it endangers, what the design or the measurement does about it, and what risk remains once that is accounted for.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

The turn-prompt buckets were constructed for this study to describe the form the user's interaction with the agent took. The three buckets of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures-process} separate a framework command from a short message and a longer one at a fifty-character threshold, a boundary set for this study rather than calibrated against an external standard. As a description of that form they measure what they were built to measure. As a stand-in for the effort behind the interaction they are weaker. A framework command is a few keystrokes for the user and can set a substantial amount of agent work in motion, while a short reply may follow a long stretch of reading. The comparison they support between the frameworks is therefore one of interaction form, and any reading of user effort taken from them carries that gap.

The cost this study reports is a price rather than a bill. Every call's tokens are priced against the published per-token rates of Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card}, which prices every session on the same terms and makes the framework comparison a comparison of what each framework consumed. It is not necessarily what the work would cost a developer to carry out. That figure would also hold the time the participant spent, which a working setting pays for at a rate no token price reflects, and whatever a solution's shortcomings cost to put right after delivery. Neither is measured here, and both can outweigh the token cost of the session that produced them. The paired differences are untouched by that, since both sides of every pair are priced identically.

The cost of a session is also a floor rather than a total, for two reasons. When a conversation compacts, the platform makes a further call to summarise the context it is about to discard. That call is not written to the transcripts the participants supply us with, so its tokens are absent from every figure here. Nine of the twenty-four sessions compacted at least once, with twelve compactions between them, and all nine fall in the Sonnet group. What is missing is concentrated on one side of the model comparison rather than spread across it. A session is also measured only to the three-hour cap of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data}, so anything after that boundary lies outside every value derived from the transcripts.

The remaining dimensions' instruments each reach a defined part of the session too, and what falls outside differs between them. The process measures locate activity on the transcript's timeline, so a participant reading the codebase, testing a build, or thinking a decision through registers only as an interval between turns. The functional measures test the running program's behaviour against the behaviour the task asks for, in the manner of assertions, so they say nothing about code quality, maintainability, security posture, or the debt a solution leaves behind. A single blocking fault also reads there as near-total failure, which Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox} shows is not hypothetical. The perception measures are single self-reported ratings standing in for constructs such as mental demand and effort, which dedicated instruments measure through several items each and which one rating cannot separate. Each dimension's headline measure carries its own claim, and none of them stands in for another.

We chose a contained replica of Open eClass over a live instance. The shared replica of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-task-environment} pins the platform to a fixed upstream baseline and seeds deterministic fixture data. Every participant develops against identical state and the grading runs against an equivalent one, which is what makes the cohort comparable and the scoring reproducible. The choice gives up any property of a live deployment the replica does not reproduce, since the task never exercises it. Such properties include timezone handling of locale-formatted dates, session-cookie behaviour under load, cross-origin interactions with the mock single-sign-on service, and upstream behaviour that has shifted since the pinned baseline.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

Each participant carried out the same task twice, so familiarity gained in the first session could lift the second and be taken for an effect of the framework. Counterbalancing the framework order exists to separate the two, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data} describes, and the realised allocation left the four assignment cells uneven. The order splits of Appendix~\ref{app:results-cost-breakdowns} and its counterparts show no order effect on any headline measure, and Section~\ref{sec:discussions-learning-effects} finds no cohort-wide direction in the total score. The evidence supports no learning effect visible in the measures; it does not establish that none occurred.

The framework comparison is paired within each participant, and the model comparison is not. Each participant kept one model across both sessions, so a difference between the Opus and the Sonnet group is a difference between six participants and six others, and it carries their skill and working style along with the model. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-analysis-plan} therefore reads every statement between the model groups descriptively.

Neither Claude Code nor the comparison framework was version-locked across the study window. Spec-kit sessions used a range of releases, which we audited release by release over the instructions installed into the project. One release changed what the agent is told, and that by a single line, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-comparison-framework} records and Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-speckit-audit} reports. What the audit establishes is that the cohort met one workflow throughout, while whether that single line shifted the sessions meeting it lies beyond its reach. Claude Code itself was updated repeatedly over the same period, so some participants' two sessions ran on different versions of it. One instance of that drift left a mark on the measures. In four Spec-kit sessions using Sonnet, the conversation accumulated context beyond a bound the other sessions could not exceed, for the reason Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics} sets out. We could not establish the cause beyond the surmise that section records, nor has Anthropic addressed it publicly to our knowledge. What that accumulation cost those sessions is priced in Table~\ref{tab:disc-above-ceiling-cost}.

Resetting a conversation was partly the participant's own decision. The platform automatically compacts a conversation as it nears its limit, but the preceding paragraph shows that reset was not always guaranteed to fire. The participants, however, could at any point compact manually, regardless of how full the context was. Spec-kit conversations under Sonnet were reset both automatically and manually by participants, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} reports. A reset changes what every following call is charged for, so where the participant chose the moment, the cost that follows reflects their judgement alongside the agent's and the framework's behaviour. The two cannot be separated here, since the same long context is both a product of how the agent works under that framework and the reason a participant resets it.

Participants knew which framework they used in each session, since blinding was not possible because the two workflows differ visibly in what they ask of the user and how the agents behave. They also knew the two were being compared. The ratings of Section~\ref{sec:results-perception} are therefore open to recall bias and to a pull towards the answer a participant expects to be wanted, and no instrument-side measurement corrects either. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-perception} reads each rating against the behaviour measured beneath it and reports the divergences as findings rather than reconciling them. That reading does not isolate the bias, since a participant answering as they believe is wanted may land either with the measures or against them, so neither agreement nor divergence identifies it.

\subsection{External Validity}

The study is conducted on one coding agent, against one task, in a setting between laboratory and field conditions. The task asks for work in two languages and research into an unfamiliar legacy codebase, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-task-specification} sets out, so it exercises a demanding end-to-end workload. It is nonetheless one task rather than a benchmark of many, and it runs against the contained environment the construct-validity discussion above describes, which fixes what the cohort meets at the price of a live deployment's variety. The findings hold within that scope. They do not reach other coding agents, work simpler or less knowledge-intensive than this task, work substantially larger than it, or purely laboratory or natural settings.

Both the wider setting of artificial intelligence and the two frameworks move quickly, and they move for different reasons. Token prices, model availability, and the pace at which agentic frameworks release belong to AI-assisted software development at large. The command sets, agent instructions, and workflows examined here belong to APM and Spec-kit, and change at each project's own pace. Every statement of either kind belongs to the study window of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-scope}. The cost values of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} are dollar equivalents priced from the rate card of Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card} rather than amounts a participant was billed, so they are defensible against that snapshot and would need re-estimating under a materially different one. The descriptive claims about how each framework behaves are tied to the releases tested.

\subsection{Reliability}

We built everything the study measures against. APM is our framework, and so are the task and the brief that the participants received, the mock environment it runs against, and the codebase the task extends. The study design is ours, as are the grader with every assertion it makes against the task's requirements and the diagnostic re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}, and the parser with every measure reported here. We also recruited the cohort. That is a condition of the study rather than a variable within it, and nothing in the design offsets it. What a reader can check rather than trust is the grading. It is fully automated, every score it reports is computed by the rubric rather than assigned by a reader, and it runs the same way on both of a participant's submissions. The task, the environment, the parser, and the grader are published, as Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-availability} lists, so the measurement and the scoring can be run again. The cohort is twelve participants drawn from university students and working professionals, a scale at which the paired design carries the comparison and no claim about a wider population is made.

The chapters read many comparisons from the same twenty-four sessions. Each dimension has one headline measure at session scope that carries its claim, while the phase, model, order, and category splits decompose that claim rather than standing beside it as further ones, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-analysis-plan} sets out. A decomposition is read descriptively and less confidently, since each covers a subset of the cohort and so rests on fewer data. Where a split sets one group of participants against another rather than a participant against themselves, it also carries whatever separates those groups. The statistics, tables, and figures are produced by the analysis scripts of Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-availability}.
