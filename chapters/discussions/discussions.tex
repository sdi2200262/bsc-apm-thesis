\section{Two Realisations of Spec-Driven Development}
\label{sec:disc-sdd}

Spec-kit's planning phase cost more than APM's in ten of the twelve pairs, the one part of the task where APM was the cheaper of the two, as the planning row of Table~\ref{tab:cost-phase} reports. The difference follows from how each framework carries out the planning of spec-driven development. Spec-kit plans more autonomously, having the agent explore the codebase and, on ambiguities, decide on best practices, drawing on the model's capabilities and training data. It fills a fixed set of template documents from what it finds, putting few or no questions to the user. APM explores the codebase as well, but it leans more on the user's input with required rounds of questions and builds review checkpoints into its planning. The gathered context from the user helps the Planner agent be more targeted and therefore more efficient. Additionally, APM creates three shorter planning documents that generalise across project types, minimizing output. The two approaches are set out where each framework is introduced, Spec-kit in Section~\ref{sec:comparison-framework} and APM in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework-design}.

The cost gap has two drivers visible in the planning phase, both pointing the same way, set out in Table~\ref{tab:planning-behaviour}. Spec-kit issued more requests to the model in every one of the twelve pairs, and in a few the gap was much wider, lifting the Hodges-Lehmann estimate of the difference well above the median. These are the extra steps of an agent that explores and drafts without waiting on the user. Spec-kit also produced more output in nine of the twelve pairs, the text that fills its larger set of documents. APM was lighter on both.

The difference points the same way in both model groups, as the per-model split in Table~\ref{tab:planning-behaviour-model} of Appendix~\ref{app:sdd-breakdowns} sets out. Spec-kit's heavier request count holds in every pair under both, and its larger output is present in both as well. The output gap widens clearly under Opus, the request gap less so. With six participants in each group the per-model intervals are wide, so the pooled comparison bears the statistical weight.

In implementation, both frameworks build against their planning documents as the source of truth and revise them as findings require, differing in the detail but not the essence. They part on cost. It is there that APM costs more, and that excess is the larger part of the overall difference, coming from how APM splits the work across several agents rather than from how it plans. Section~\ref{sec:disc-cost-mechanics} takes that up.

\section{Cost Mechanics of APM and Spec-kit}
\label{sec:disc-cost-mechanics}

APM's higher cost was set in implementation. Its planning cost less than Spec-kit's, as established in Section~\ref{sec:disc-sdd}, but its execution of the plan was more expensive, by a margin wide enough to outweigh that saving. Table~\ref{tab:cost-phase} sets out both phase cost differences. The cause is structural; Spec-kit works within a single main agent conversation, the one that also planned, whereas an APM implementation phase works with at least three additional conversations: a Manager and two Workers. Each conversation built its own cache, produced its own output, and accumulated its own context, so APM's spending exceeded Spec-kit's in the output and cache-write categories, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token} details. Those are the most expensive of the five token categories, as the rate card in Appendix~\ref{app:rate-card} sets out, so APM's excess fell on the costliest tokens. That cost is therefore a by-product of APM's workload distribution.

The two model groups did not bear this cost in the same way, and the difference lies in the size of each model's context window and how the platform handles a conversation that reaches its limit. As Section~\ref{sec:context-windows-and-context-decay} describes, each call costs more than the last as the context grows, and a compaction resets it to a low starting point. In Claude Code, compactions happen automatically as a conversation nears its limit, and the user may also trigger one manually. APM has its own form of this reset, the Handoff described in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework-design}, which in cost terms works the same way as a compaction. As Table~\ref{tab:compaction} reports, such resets happened in only one of the two model groups.

The Sonnet 4.6 model used in our study had a two-hundred-thousand-token context window. Conversations often grew near its ceiling and reset, either auto-compacted by the platform or compacted manually by the participant. A Sonnet conversation that compacted therefore used more than one context window, clearing its cache and building its context anew. Opus 4.7, by contrast, had a one-million-token context window, large enough to hold the entire task from start to finish.

Figure~\ref{fig:occupancy-pooled} plots the peak each context window reached across the cohort. A conversation that compacted twice, for example, resolves to three peaks, one for each context window it used. The highest markers are Spec-kit conversations that grew long without a reset. APM's conversations generally sit below them, most in a dense low cluster, since dividing the work leaves each agent with less context to accumulate.

A few APM markers nonetheless reach the high ranges, one even passing the Spec-kit conversation it is paired with. These are all APM Workers, and they accumulate the largest amount of tokens of any APM role, therefore contributing the largest part of its cost, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:cost-role}.

Under Opus no conversation reset, so each used only one context window, growing from the start of the task to its end. Each call is charged for the whole context before it, and APM's conversations together held more accumulated context than Spec-kit's one. Therefore, APM with Opus paid more, even on cache reads, the one category where dividing the work might have lowered spending instead. With higher cache writes and output on top, APM cost more under Opus in every token category but input, where the difference was negligible. Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-model} presents this and the wide gaps of Figure~\ref{fig:occupancy-opus} show Spec-kit holding a few lone high markers and APM a spread that reaches toward them.

Under Sonnet, both frameworks faced the tighter window's pressure. Spec-kit's conversation reached the limit and compacted in five of its six sessions, more than once in three. Every APM session had one reset as well; all six facing the context window limits with the Worker assigned to C part of the task, its heaviest work. Four auto-compacted, as Table~\ref{tab:compaction} reports, and the other two reset through a Worker Handoff instead, which the table's compaction tally does not capture.

In cost terms a Spec-kit conversation that compacted behaved as a succession of agents, each starting afresh in a new context window, rebuilding cache and climbing again, which is why under Sonnet its cost came closer to APM's. This shows in the cache reading. Under Sonnet, Spec-kit read more from the cache than APM, the reverse of the Opus case, as the cache-read row of Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-model} reports. Figure~\ref{fig:occupancy-sonnet} shows why. Spec-kit drove one conversation up to the compaction ceiling and back, paid for a large context on each climb, while APM held most of its conversations well below the ceiling, where each call had to reread less.

A platform issue we observed during the study complicates the Sonnet reading. A model cannot serve a request past its context window, and Claude Code auto-compacts near the limit to prevent reaching it. In four Spec-kit sessions, with every indicator reporting that same model, the conversation passed the auto-compaction ceiling without resetting and went on serving requests past the window. This shows in the highest points of Figure~\ref{fig:occupancy-sonnet}. These sessions appear to have been served by a larger variant the platform never surfaced. The issue followed the session's Claude Code version, not its framework, but its mark on accumulation shows only in Spec-kit, since no APM session passed the window. That added accumulation plausibly lifted Spec-kit's Sonnet cache reads.

The issue bears little on the cost reading described above. The added accumulation resulted in more cache reading, the cheapest of the token categories. Impacted conversations went only modestly past the auto-compaction ceiling in all but one session, which went about a hundred thousand tokens over. Including this one, three of the four then compacted manually and continued in a new context window; the same behaviour as the Spec-kit and APM sessions that did not meet the issue. The fourth never reset, and accumulated to about fifty thousand tokens over the auto-compaction ceiling. We cannot know how it would have continued had its context been compacted instead. Section~\ref{sec:threats} treats this as a threat to validity.

\section{Learning Effects}
\label{sec:disc-learning-effects}

The confined number of sessions in the case study allows each participant's pair to be examined individually for learning carried from the first session into the second. The workspace and the agent are reset between sessions, so the participant is the only carrier of that memory. The evidence is sought in their own inputs: a second-session instruction that pre-empts an error the first session made, an explicit reference to an earlier decision, or a detail handled correctly from recall rather than rediscovered. A stronger second-session result alone does not count, since the agent may have produced it unaided. These signs are read case by case, as indications of learning rather than a conclusive measure of it.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

This section enumerates the internal, external, and construct validity threats and the mitigations applied. Each threat points back to the specific section that motivates it.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

\textbf{Learning effects from the within-subjects design.} The within-subjects design declared in the Case-Study Design (Section~\ref{sec:case-study-design}) has each participant complete the same task twice, so task familiarity from the first session can influence performance in the second. Counterbalancing the framework order across participants, as described in Sessions and Data (Section~\ref{sec:sessions-data}), mitigates the framework-effect confound but does not eliminate the underlying learning curve. The learning-effects examination described in Section~\ref{sec:disc-learning-effects} reads each participant's two sessions for evidence of carried-over learning and is the primary defence against this threat.

\textbf{Self-reported versus observed measures.} The perception measures described in Section~\ref{sec:measures} pair the instrumented, transcript-derived measures with self-reported workload, confidence, and perceived framework effectiveness; observed divergences between self-report and instrument are reported as findings rather than absorbed as an unstated limitation. Residual risk remains: self-reports are subject to recall bias and social-desirability pressures that the instrument-side measurements cannot independently correct.

\subsection{External Validity}

\textbf{Generalisation scope.} The case study is conducted on a single AI coding CLI, against a complex software engineering task requiring two-language implementation and legacy-codebase research, described in the Task Specification (Section~\ref{sec:task-spec}), in a setting positioned between laboratory and field conditions. Findings generalise within this scope and do not generalise to multi-platform settings, dynamic cross-model routing, simpler or less knowledge-intensive tasks, or pure laboratory or natural-setting conditions.

\textbf{Temporal decay of findings.} The AI assistant field is evolving rapidly. Effect sizes and descriptive claims throughout this chapter (rate-card prices, command sets, framework releases, and model availability) are tied to the May 2026 snapshot stated in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}. The dollar-equivalent cost values reported under Chapter~\ref{chap:results} rest on the rate-card snapshot and are defensible against it, but may require re-estimation under a materially different rate card.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

\textbf{Behavioural input buckets as process-efficiency proxy.} The short, protocol, and standard input buckets defined under the Process measures in Section~\ref{sec:measures} are extracted from the transcript content for this study. They are not validated against an external coordination-burden instrument. Their validity as proxies for human coordination effort rests on face validity alone: the relative ordering of frameworks along these metrics is robust, but the absolute magnitude of differences is interpretive rather than calibrated.

\textbf{Instrument coverage limits.} The 100-point rubric captures correctness and robustness but not code quality, maintainability, security posture, or long-term technical debt. Metric extraction is further limited to the Claude Code JSONL transcripts and their sibling subagent files that accompany each participant submission, as declared in Sessions and Data (Section~\ref{sec:sessions-data}). Any interaction with the AI that took place outside the CLI's transcript surface is invisible to the metric set.

\textbf{Replica versus live Open eClass.} The shared Docker-based replica of the Open eClass platform described in the Task Environment (Section~\ref{sec:task-env}) pins the platform to a fixed upstream baseline and seeds deterministic fixture data. The shared replica makes the cohort comparable and the grading reproducible, and removes any parity gap between what participants build against and what the evaluation runs against. The trade-off is that the task is performed against a replica rather than against any live university instance of Open eClass. Any property of a live instance that the replica does not reproduce is not exercised by the case study. Such properties include timezone handling of locale-formatted dates, session-cookie behaviour under load, cross-origin interactions with the mock CAS service, and upstream behaviours that have shifted since the baseline commit.
