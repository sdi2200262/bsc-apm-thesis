\section{Two Realisations of Spec-Driven Planning}
\label{sec:discussions-sdd}

Both frameworks plan and create specifications before they build, and both explore the codebase and dispatch subagents for the heavier research. They differ in who makes the planning decisions. Spec-kit has the agent resolve the open questions itself, making informed guesses based on the model's training data. It fills a fixed set of template documents from what it finds and knows, and it turns to the user only through an optional step that offers recommended choices to confirm. Its planning is therefore sensitive to the model's capability, and how the autonomous exploration will play out. APM keeps the user as the authority. Its Planner goes through a series of question rounds with the user and brings back what it understood for approval before any document is written. It is directed to ask rather than assume, and it produces a few short planning documents that generalise across project types. The task brief the participants worked from was already detailed, yet under it APM still asked many targeted questions grounded in its research, while Spec-kit, with the same brief, asked little. Both gate their planning and ask the user for approval. APM's gates are points where the user approves each planning document, while Spec-kit's are the agent's own validation checks and a holistic presentation to the user at the end.

The planning turns differed in kind accordingly, as the planning buckets of Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition} set out. Spec-kit, planning mostly on its own, turned to the user to confirm recommended choices and approve them rather than to answer open questions, while APM brought the user's decisions into the planning, requiring more substantive interactions.

That difference in authority does not reach the size of the planning phase, which holds no firm difference in cost, duration, or turn count, as the planning rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-phase-paired}, Table~\ref{tab:proc-phase-paired}, and Table~\ref{tab:proc-turns-paired} report. What differs is where the planning work took place, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-planning-behaviour-paired} sets out. Under Spec-kit the main agent did that work itself, issuing more requests to the model in eleven of the twelve pairs and making more tool calls in the same eleven. Under APM part of it was offloaded to subagents, which made more tool calls than Spec-kit's in ten of the twelve pairs.

Both frameworks had sessions that fell well short. Five across the cohort scored below fifty in the distribution of Section~\ref{sec:results-functional}, two of them APM's and three Spec-kit's, so the shortfall was not one framework's alone. They did not all fail in the same way. The re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox} lifts a common fault under either framework, and from it both of APM's recover along with one of Spec-kit's, which leaves two Spec-kit sessions still below fifty. In Spec-kit's largely autonomous planning, a step that settles an open question without the user can do so wrongly, and the build then rests on that decision. APM puts its open questions to the user in the Planner's question rounds, and it holds each planning document at a review gate. A wrong assumption therefore meets the user before the build inherits it. Those two remaining sessions line up with that difference in planning authority, but they do not establish that the one produced the other.

\section{Coordinating the Implementation}
\label{sec:discussions-implementation}

Both frameworks build against their planning documents and revise them as findings require. Spec-kit carries the implementation out in the same agent that also planned. In APM, a Manager and its Workers carry it out across several agents and coordinate through the user. The several agents shape what the implementation costs, and that coordination shapes what it demands, read in the two subsections below.

\subsection{The Cost of Several Agents}
\label{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics}

Under per-token pricing, each of APM's added conversations built its own cache, produced its own output, and accumulated its own context, so APM's spending exceeded Spec-kit's in the output and cache-write categories, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired} details. Those are the most expensive of the five token categories, as the rate card in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card} sets out, so APM's excess fell on the costliest tokens. APM's higher overall cost rests on this implementation excess alone, since the planning phase had no firm cost difference, as the two phase rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-phase-paired} set out. That cost is, however, a by-product of working across several agents, not an aim of it.

The two model groups did not bear this cost in the same way, and the difference lies in the size of each model's context window and how the platform handles a conversation that reaches its limit. As Section~\ref{sec:background-context-windows-and-context-decay} describes, each call costs more than the last as the context grows, and a compaction resets it to a low starting point. In Claude Code, compactions happen automatically as a conversation nears its limit, and the user may also trigger one manually. APM has its own form of this reset, the Handoff described in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework-design}, which in cost terms works the same way as a compaction. As Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} reports, such resets happened in only one of the two model groups.

The Sonnet 4.6 model used in our study had a two-hundred-thousand-token context window. Conversations often grew near its ceiling and reset, either auto-compacted by the platform or compacted manually by the participant. A Sonnet conversation that compacted therefore used more than one context window, clearing its cache and building its context anew. Opus 4.7, by contrast, had a one-million-token context window, large enough to hold the entire task from start to finish.

Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions} plots the peak each context window reached across the cohort. A conversation that compacted twice, for example, resolves to three peaks, one for each context window it used. The highest markers are Spec-kit conversations that grew long without a reset. APM's conversations generally sit below them, most in a dense low cluster, since dividing the work leaves each agent with less context to accumulate.

A few APM markers nonetheless reach the high ranges, one even passing the Spec-kit conversation it is paired with. These are all APM Workers, and they accumulate the largest amount of tokens of any APM role, therefore contributing the largest part of its cost, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:cost-session-levels-by-role}.

Under Opus, all three paired summaries were positive for output and both cache-write categories. Input was negligible, while the cache-read summaries were positive despite one negative pair. The cache-write differences were positive in every pair, while output and cache-read differences were positive in five of six. Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-model} presents this and the wide gaps of Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-opus} show Spec-kit holding a few lone high markers and APM a spread that reaches toward them.

Under Sonnet, both frameworks faced the tighter window's pressure. Spec-kit's conversation reached the limit and compacted in five of its six sessions, more than once in three. In cost terms a Spec-kit conversation that compacted behaved as a succession of agents, each starting fresh in a new context window, rebuilding cache and accumulating context again, which is why under Sonnet its cost came closer to APM's. This shows in the cache reading. Under Sonnet, Spec-kit read more from the cache than APM, the reverse of the Opus case, as the cache-read row of Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-model} reports. \subsection{The Demands on the User}
\label{sec:discussions-demands}

APM keeps the user in control of the build, just as it does of the plan. Its design routes implementation through the user, and the authority over the work stays with them rather than the agent. Spec-kit extends its planning autonomy into implementation, where its agent works through the task list largely on its own. The greater demand APM places on the user is therefore by design, the same authority it provides during planning, while Spec-kit continues with minimal interaction.

That difference appears in the user's input. APM has far more turn prompts than Spec-kit, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-turns-levels} reports. In implementation, most of APM's prompts are protocol, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition} reports. These protocol prompts are the per-cycle relay APM's design places in the user's hands, each being a dispatch out or a report back rather than an instruction. Standard prompts are few under both frameworks, and what little APM has reaches the Manager rather than the Workers, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition-by-role} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-process-breakdowns} sets out. APM's added demand is therefore coordination, not deliberation.

Spec-kit asks much less. Its few turns are the protocol command that launches the implementation and an occasional short or standard follow-up, too few to read as a settled composition. The agent proceeds largely on its own, and the user has little to do until the build is done.

Because APM has several agents carry out its implementation where Spec-kit has one, it has a naturally larger model request count. APM issued more requests than Spec-kit in every one of the twelve pairs, and its main agents made more tool calls in all twelve as well, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-implementation-behaviour-paired} reports. Both reverse the planning phase, where Spec-kit's main agent led on the two measures. Each agent additionally produces its own output, which is part of why APM's implementation cost the more of the two, as the cost reading of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics} sets out.

The cost is a by-product of the design, while the demand is its aim, keeping the user in control of the build where Spec-kit leaves it mostly to the agent.

\section{Behind the Functional Score}
\label{sec:discussions-blackbox}

The functional results take the grader's scores at face value. This section looks behind them, first at what a black-box grader can miss and then at what the scores cost.

\subsection{Grading with a Black Box}
\label{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}

The grader judges a submission only by the behaviour of the running program, never its source, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures-functional} sets out. A single fault that blocks the program's responses therefore reads as near-total failure, even where the rest of the implementation may be sound.

Three Sonnet sessions show this. Each scored exactly 34 on the total, with the Python half complete and almost the whole C half lost. The cause in all three was the same narrow flaw in the parser their C replica used to read MCP messages. MCP carries requests as JSON-RPC objects\footnote{MCP transport specification available at: \url{https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/transports}}, so the replica had to read each JSON object and decide which operation to perform. Our grader sends requests with ordinary spacing between JSON fields, while an alternative compact form removes those spaces\footnote{For example, \texttt{\{"id": 1\}} uses ordinary spacing, while \texttt{\{"id":1\}} is compact.}. Both forms spell the same object, since the whitespace between fields is insignificant in the JSON the protocol uses.\footnote{RFC 8259, Section 2 (\url{https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8259}).}

A conformant parser should accept both forms. The affected parsers accepted only the compact spelling. Because the first grader request used the spaced form, their C parser was unresponsive and the exchange stopped before the later checks could probe the rest of the implementation. These attempts were otherwise strong. When graded again with the grader's requests being in compact form, the three sessions received diagnostic scores between 93.9 and 100. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-grading-compact-comparison} plots the full Sonnet group, and Table~\ref{tab:disc-grading-compact-sessions} gives the three changed scores.

Five sessions across the cohort scored below fifty. The re-grade recovers three of them, both of APM's and one of Spec-kit's. After it, APM's sessions are all high, and the two that stay below fifty are Spec-kit's. What those two are, this re-grade cannot say. It reaches one kind of grader strictness, and the two it leaves may be sound work it does not reach, just as the three it recovered were, or they may be genuine shortfalls. Recovering two APM sessions also moves the cohort comparison toward APM, and one participant carries that shift alone. P010 shipped the flaw in the APM session only, so the re-grade lifts that session and their pair moves from 4.4 to 64.3 points. P003 shipped it in both sessions, so the re-grade lifts both and their pair moves only from zero to five points. The flaw appeared under both frameworks, so it is not one framework's failing.

This re-grade is therefore a diagnostic, not a fairer score. It reaches one fault class, while the total score stays the basis of the comparison. The grader can hide sound work in other forms this one re-grade does not show, so it bounds, rather than measures, what the scores leave out.

\subsection{Cost versus Score}
\label{sec:discussions-cost-score}

The two frameworks scored level across the cohort, as Section~\ref{sec:results-functional} reports, while APM cost more, as Section~\ref{sec:results-economic} reports. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-cost-score-comparison} sets the two differences against each other. Most participants sit at a slightly positive to no score difference with a positive cost difference. For them APM cost more and the functional outcome was the same. The cost gap is wide under Opus and slight under Sonnet.

The three participants whose APM session scored clearly above their Spec-kit one are the exception. Their APM session cost about the same or a little less, not more. At three sessions this is an observation rather than a result.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/discussions/cost_score.pdf}
  \caption{Per-participant paired cost difference and total-score difference, APM minus Spec-kit, marked by model. The reference lines mark no difference on each axis.}
  \label{fig:disc-cost-score-comparison}

What the added cost bought was APM's structure and the user's control over the build, which on this task did not register in the functional score. The task proved to be bounded and self-contained. One agent, with any subagents it dispatched, could carry it largely on its own, so APM's heavier structure had little functional gap to close. Whether that structure earns its cost is beyond what a task of this size can show. Its value would lie in what the study cannot measure here: the control it keeps in the user's hands, the user's sovereignty of the software produced and in work larger or longer than the one tested.

\section{Perceived Versus Measured Behaviour}
\label{sec:discussions-perception}

Section~\ref{sec:results-perception} reports each rating's sign and distribution on its own terms. This section reads them against the measured behaviour underneath, including the value Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-score} closed by hedging as beyond the study's harder instruments: the control the user keeps over the build. What follows reads whether self-report validates the measured outcome or diverges from it, and what that divergence exposes about what the instrument set cannot see.

Effectiveness and confidence diverge from the total score outright. Both favour APM by a wide margin, with no participant rating Spec-kit higher on either, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports. Against that, the total score sits on par between the frameworks, with a paired difference of half a point and a confidence interval spanning zero, as Section~\ref{sec:results-functional} reports. The preference is genuine, and it does not track delivered outcome.

What aligns instead is the perception of the process the user took part in. One participant, who used APM in their second session, wrote that they were ``much more confident in APMs output'' despite ``not having manually checked either of the code output'', naming the reason as the framework being ``strict as to enforcing the PRD when speckit was more lenient''. Confidence here reads as trust in a specification the participant helped settle during planning, rather than a judgement of code they verified, echoing the reading Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd} already builds from the score point of view. The Spec-kit side of the same divergence names the absence directly. One other participant wrote that they ``felt lack of control when it came to the deliverables and how they were produced'', and another found it odd that the framework did not operate interactively, letting the agent take its own decisions with the user left out entirely.

Effort is the one item where APM is behind. It was the more effortful framework for a slight majority of participants, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports, but evidently that effort does not track where APM adds turns. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-demands} reads APM's extra implementation turns as the intended user-mediated coordination. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd} reads planning turns the other way. Turn count there sits level between the frameworks, but APM's are the more substantive ones, since its Planner brings real open questions to the user while gathering context. One participant's account matches this directly, writing that ``the most demanding thing was the planning, because there were questions about the whole plan in advance to clarify almost everything about the implementation'', after which ``the whole task was executed by the workers easily without any assistance from me, other than authorizing terminal commands and checking stuff to see if it aligns with the task''.

Steering confirms the same reading. Delivered steers stayed near zero under both frameworks, as Section~\ref{sec:results-process-interaction} reports. Frustration also points the same way. APM was still rated the less frustrating framework, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports, despite the added time and turns, so the added burden did not compound into felt friction.

Mental demand and the effort spent checking the AI's output have no measured counterpart at all. That is a deliberate finding. Some of the cognitive work that AI-assisted development demands leaves almost no trace in the instrument set, which is exactly the gap that self-reporting attempts to fill. One participant's account of a Spec-kit session is the clearest instance: ``I didn't have a way to validate the results of the session despite taking the agents word which run tests with the mock instance'' and ``I didn't do my due diligence in reviewing the code output''. That session's total score is the lowest in the cohort, so here the participant's own doubt and the measured outcome point the same way.

\section{Learning Effects}
\label{sec:discussions-learning-effects}

The within-subjects design has each participant carry out the same task twice, with the environment reset between the two sessions so that no code, workspace state, or assistant context passes from the first into the second. Anything a participant took from their first attempt could then lift their second, and left unaccounted it would be indistinguishable from an effect of the framework used. This section reads the cohort for such a carried-over effect, holding to the same reproducible standard as the rest of the chapter and setting transcript reading aside.

That standard fixes what evidence the question can draw on. The chapter measures each session along four dimensions, and the score is not the only one that could in principle bear on learning. How a participant interacted with the framework and its agents, the interaction measures of Section~\ref{sec:results-process-interaction}, might shift as the task grew familiar. The obstacle is that the comparison here is within participant, between two sessions that used different frameworks, and the two demand such different interaction that the difference between a participant's two sessions is already large from the change of framework alone. A learning-driven change would mesh into that framework difference rather than standing apart. The cost a session incurred and the time it took stand the same way, each shaped first by the framework. The score is different in kind, being the task's graded outcome on one rubric rather than framework-shaped behaviour, so a within-participant difference is a common measure a practiced participant should raise. We therefore read the score alone to investigate learning effects.

One limit still bounds what the score can show of a learning effect: the rubric's ceiling. Most sessions scored near the hundred-point maximum, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-levels} reports, and nine of the twelve participants already scored above ninety in their first session, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-sessions} details, so a practiced second session had almost no room to register a gain and only three participants entered their second with substantial ground to recover.

The individual changes have no common direction. Four participants scored higher in their second session, four scored lower, and four had the same score, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-learning-by-participant} sets out. Nine changes lay between a two-point loss and a six-point gain. Of the three larger movements, P002 gained 53 points while P001 and P012 lost 23.4 and 12.3 points. These movements all favour APM regardless of its position, so they cannot separate a practice effect from the framework or from session-to-session variation.

P010 has a different reading under the diagnostic re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}. The first-session diagnostic score remains $29.6$ while the second rises to $93.9$. The two sessions therefore differ by $64.3$ points on the diagnostic score but only $4.4$ on the total. The second session's diagnostic score demonstrates that this re-grade recovers a strong implementation. Since the re-grade reaches only one fault class, however, the first session's unchanged diagnostic score cannot establish that the first implementation was unsound. The diagnostic difference therefore remains unable to separate learning from the change of framework.

The order split supports the same cautious reading. Table~\ref{tab:func-score-paired-by-order} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-functional-breakdowns} separates the paired total-score difference by which framework each participant met first, and both confidence intervals cross zero. Counterbalancing distributed first exposure across the frameworks, although the realised seven-to-five allocation was not perfectly even.

The total scores therefore offer no clear cohort-wide learning direction. The individual changes are mixed, and the score ceiling limits what improvement could appear. The slightly uneven order allocation is a secondary qualification rather than the source of that uncertainty. These results cannot confirm or measure a learning effect for any one participant.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussions-threats}

Each threat below states what it endangers, what the design or the measurement does about it, and what risk remains once that is accounted for. Each also points back to the section that motivates it.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

The turn-prompt buckets were constructed for this study to describe the form the user's interaction with the agent took. The three buckets of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures-process} separate a framework command from a short message and a longer one at a fifty-character threshold, a boundary set for this study rather than calibrated against an external standard. As a description of that form they measure what they were built to measure. As a stand-in for the effort behind the interaction they are weaker, since a framework command is a few keystrokes for the user and can set a substantial amount of agent work in motion, while a short reply may follow a long stretch of reading. The comparison they support between the frameworks is therefore one of interaction form, and any reading of user effort taken from them carries that gap.

The cost this study reports is a price rather than a bill. Every call's tokens are priced against the published per-token rates of Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card}, which prices every session on the same terms and makes the framework comparison a comparison of what each framework consumed. It is not necessarily what the work would cost a developer to carry out. That figure would also hold the time the participant spent, which a working setting pays for at a rate no token price reflects, and whatever a solution's shortcomings cost to put right after delivery. Neither is measured here, and both can outweigh the token cost of the session that produced them. The paired differences are untouched by that, since both sides of every pair are priced identically.

The cost of a session is also a floor rather than a total, for two reasons. When a conversation compacts, the platform makes a further call to summarise the context it is about to discard, and that call is not written to the transcripts the participants supply us with, so its tokens are absent from every figure here. Nine of the twenty-four sessions compacted at least once, with twelve compactions between them, and all nine fall in the Sonnet group, so what is missing is concentrated on one side of the model comparison rather than spread across it. A session is also measured only to the three-hour cap of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data}, so anything after that boundary lies outside every value derived from the transcripts.
The remaining dimensions' instruments each reach a defined part of the session too, and what falls outside differs between them. The process measures locate activity on the transcript's timeline, so a participant reading the codebase, testing a build, or thinking a decision through registers only as an interval between turns. The functional measures test the running program's behaviour against the behaviour the task asks for, in the manner of assertions, so they say nothing about code quality, maintainability, security posture, or the debt a solution leaves behind. A single blocking fault also reads there as near-total failure, which Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox} shows is not hypothetical. The perception measures are single self-reported ratings standing in for constructs such as mental demand and effort, which dedicated instruments measure through several items each and which one rating cannot separate. Each dimension's headline measure carries its own claim, and none of them stands in for another.

The task was carried out against a contained replica of Open eClass rather than a live instance, and that was the design's choice. The shared replica of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-task-environment} pins the platform to a fixed upstream baseline and seeds deterministic fixture data, so every participant develops against identical state and the grading runs against an equivalent one, which is what makes the cohort comparable and the scoring reproducible. What the choice gives up is any property of a live deployment the replica does not reproduce, since the task never exercises it. Such properties include timezone handling of locale-formatted dates, session-cookie behaviour under load, cross-origin interactions with the mock single-sign-on service, and upstream behaviour that has shifted since the pinned baseline.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

Each participant carried out the same task twice, so familiarity gained in the first session could lift the second, and left unaccounted such a gain would be indistinguishable from an effect of the framework. Counterbalancing the framework order exists to separate the two, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data} describes, and the realised order groups had seven participants APM first against five, which left the four assignment cells uneven. The order splits of Appendix~\ref{app:results-cost-breakdowns} and its counterparts show no order effect on any headline measure, and Section~\ref{sec:discussions-learning-effects} finds no cohort-wide direction in the total score. What the evidence supports is that no learning effect is visible in the measures, not that none occurred.

The framework comparison is paired within each participant, and the model comparison is not. Each participant kept one model across both sessions, so a difference between the Opus and the Sonnet group is a difference between six participants and six others, and it carries their skill and working style along with the model. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-analysis-plan} therefore reads every statement between the model groups descriptively.

Neither Claude Code nor the comparison framework was version-locked across the study window. Spec-kit sessions used a range of releases, which we audited release by release over the instructions installed into the project. One release changed what the agent is told, and that by a single line, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-comparison-framework} records and Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-speckit-audit} reports. What the audit establishes is that the cohort met one workflow throughout, while whether that single line shifted the sessions meeting it lies beyond its reach. Claude Code itself was updated repeatedly over the same period, so some participants' two sessions ran on different versions of it. One instance of that drift left a mark on the measures. In four Spec-kit sessions using Sonnet the conversation was served past the context window stated for the selected model without the platform's automatic reset firing, as Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics} sets out, and accumulated context beyond a bound the other sessions could not exceed. We could not establish the cause beyond the surmise that section records, nor has Anthropic addressed it publicly to our knowledge. What that accumulation cost those sessions is priced in Table~\ref{tab:disc-above-ceiling-cost}.

Resetting a conversation was partly the participant's own decision. The platform automatically compacts a conversation as it nears its limit, but the preceding paragraph shows that reset was not always guaranteed to fire. The participants, however, could at any point compact manually, regardless of how full the context was. Spec-kit conversations under Sonnet were reset both automatically and manually by participants, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics} reports. A reset changes what every following call is charged for, so where the participant chose the moment, the cost that follows reflects their judgement alongside the agent's and the framework's behaviour. The two cannot be separated here, since the same long context is both a product of how the agent works under that framework and the reason a participant resets it.

Participants knew which framework they used in each session, since blinding was not possible because the two workflows differ visibly in what they ask of the user and how the agents behave. They also knew the two were being compared. The ratings of Section~\ref{sec:results-perception} are therefore open to recall bias and to a pull towards the answer a participant expects to be wanted, and no instrument-side measurement corrects either. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-perception} reads each rating against the behaviour measured beneath it and reports the divergences as findings rather than reconciling them. That reading does not isolate the bias, since a participant answering as they believe is wanted may land either with the measures or against them, so neither agreement nor divergence identifies it.

\subsection{External Validity}

The study is conducted on one coding agent, against one task, in a setting between laboratory and field conditions. The task asks for work in two languages and research into an unfamiliar legacy codebase, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-task-specification} sets out, so it exercises a demanding end-to-end workload. It is nonetheless one task rather than a benchmark of many, and it runs against the contained environment the construct-validity discussion above describes, which fixes what the cohort meets at the price of a live deployment's variety. The findings hold within that scope. They do not reach other coding agents, work simpler or less knowledge-intensive than this task, work substantially larger than it, or purely laboratory or natural settings.

Both the wider setting of artificial intelligence and the two frameworks move quickly, and they move for different reasons. Token prices, model availability, and the pace at which agentic frameworks release belong to AI-assisted software development at large, while the command sets, agent instructions, and workflows examined here belong to APM and Spec-kit and change at each project's own pace. Every statement of either kind belongs to the study window of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-scope}. The cost values of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} are dollar equivalents priced from the rate card of Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card} rather than amounts a participant was billed, so they are defensible against that snapshot and would need re-estimating under a materially different one. The descriptive claims about how each framework behaves are tied to the releases tested.

\subsection{Reliability}

We built everything the study measures against. APM is our framework, and so are the task and the brief that the participants received, the mock environment it runs against, the codebase the task extends, the study design, the grader together with every assertion it makes against the task's requirements and the diagnostic re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}, and the parser together with every measure reported here. We also recruited the cohort. That is a condition of the study rather than a variable within it, and nothing in the design offsets it. What a reader can check rather than trust is the grading. It is fully automated, every score it reports is computed by the rubric rather than assigned by a reader, and it runs the same way on both of a participant's submissions. The task, the environment, the parser, and the grader are published, as Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-availability} lists, so the measurement and the scoring can be run again. The cohort is twelve participants drawn from university students and working professionals, a scale at which the paired design carries the comparison and no claim about a wider population is made.

The chapters read many comparisons from the same twenty-four sessions. Each dimension has one headline measure at session scope that carries its claim, while the phase, model, order, and category splits decompose that claim rather than standing beside it as further ones, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-analysis-plan} sets out. A decomposition is read descriptively and less confidently, since each covers a subset of the cohort and so rests on fewer data. Where a split sets one group of participants against another rather than a participant against themselves, it also carries whatever separates those groups. The statistics, tables, and figures are produced by the analysis scripts of Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-availability}.
