\section{Two Realisations of Spec-Driven Planning}
\label{sec:discussions-sdd}

Both frameworks plan and create specifications before they build, and both explore the codebase and dispatch subagents for the heavier research. They differ in who makes the planning decisions. Spec-kit has the agent resolve the open questions itself, making informed guesses based on best practice and the model's training. It fills a fixed set of template documents from what it finds and knows, and it turns to the user only through an optional step that offers recommended choices to confirm. Its planning is therefore sensitive to the model's capability, and how the autonomous exploration will play out. APM keeps the user as the authority. Its Planner goes through a series of question rounds with the user and brings back what it understood for approval before any document is written. It is directed to ask rather than assume, and it produces a few short planning documents that generalise across project types. The task brief the participants worked from was already detailed, yet under it APM still asked many targeted questions grounded in its research, while Spec-kit, with the same brief, asked little. Both gate their planning and ask the user for approval. APM's gates are points where the user approves each planning document, while Spec-kit's are the agent's own validation checks and a holistic presentation to the user at the end.

The planning phase is therefore where the frameworks diverge in realising spec-driven development. The divergence is reflected in the results that follow, taken in turn, and reflected first in cost. Spec-kit's planning phase cost more than APM's, the one part of the task where APM was the cheaper of the two, as the planning row of Table~\ref{tab:cost-phase-paired} reports. The cost gap has two drivers visible in the planning phase, both pointing the same way, set out in Table~\ref{tab:disc-planning-behaviour-paired}. Spec-kit issued more requests to the model in every one of the twelve pairs, and in a few the gap was much wider, pulling the Hodges-Lehmann estimate of the difference further from zero than the median. These are the extra steps of an agent that explores and drafts without waiting on the user. Spec-kit also produced more output in nine of the twelve pairs, the text that fills its larger set of documents. APM was lighter on both.

In planning the duration leaned toward Spec-kit being the longer of the two, but its interval spans zero, so the phase has no firm difference in total time. The firm contrast is in the active time, where Spec-kit's agent worked longer, as the planning turn-time row of Table~\ref{tab:proc-phase-paired} reports, within about the same number of turns as APM, shown in Table~\ref{tab:proc-turns-levels}. That extra working time is the heavier request count and output behind the cost difference, set out in Table~\ref{tab:disc-planning-behaviour-paired} above. It is the same autonomous exploration, now seen as time rather than as model calls. Spec-kit's time therefore went mostly into the agent's own work, while APM's fell relatively more into the idle between turns, reported in the inter-turn idle row of Table~\ref{tab:proc-phase-paired}. That idle difference is small, and the larger active-time difference is what sets the phase's slight lean toward Spec-kit.

Those turns also differed in kind, as the planning buckets of Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition} show. Within them Spec-kit planned mostly on its own, turning to the user to confirm recommended choices and approve rather than to answer open questions, while APM brought the user's decisions into the planning. APM's longer standard messages take more time to read and write, consistent with its slightly higher idle between turns, though that idle is not itself a measure of deliberation.

The difference reaches the functional outcomes in the lower tail of the score distribution. Spec-kit accounts for most of it, as Section~\ref{sec:results-functional} reports. A planning step that settles an open question without the user can settle it wrongly, and the build then rests on that decision, while APM's question rounds give the user a chance to correct it first. However, the reading is only consistent with the difference in planning authority rather than proof of it.

Both frameworks had sessions that scored low. APM's two came from a shared format fault, and the re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}, which lifts that fault under either framework, leaves every APM session high. Spec-kit's low scores are read there too, where its real failures are told apart from the rest.

\section{Coordinating the Implementation}
\label{sec:discussions-implementation}

Both frameworks build against their planning documents and revise them as findings require. Spec-kit carries the implementation out in the same agent that also planned. In APM, a Manager and its Workers carry it out across several agents and coordinate through the user. The several agents shape what the implementation costs, and that coordination shapes what it demands, read in the two subsections below.

\subsection{The Cost of Several Agents}
\label{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics}

Under per-token pricing, each of APM's added conversations built its own cache, produced its own output, and accumulated its own context, so APM's spending exceeded Spec-kit's in the output and cache-write categories, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired} details. Those are the most expensive of the five token categories, as the rate card in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-rate-card} sets out, so APM's excess fell on the costliest tokens. This implementation cost is the larger part of the overall difference, and it outweighs the planning saving of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd}, as the two phase rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost-phase-paired} set out, so APM cost more overall. That cost is a by-product of working across several agents, not an aim of it.

The two model groups did not bear this cost in the same way, and the difference lies in the size of each model's context window and how the platform handles a conversation that reaches its limit. As Section~\ref{sec:background-context-windows-and-context-decay} describes, each call costs more than the last as the context grows, and a compaction resets it to a low starting point. In Claude Code, compactions happen automatically as a conversation nears its limit, and the user may also trigger one manually. APM has its own form of this reset, the Handoff described in Chapter~\ref{chap:framework-design}, which in cost terms works the same way as a compaction. As Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} reports, such resets happened in only one of the two model groups.

The Sonnet 4.6 model used in our study had a two-hundred-thousand-token context window. Conversations often grew near its ceiling and reset, either auto-compacted by the platform or compacted manually by the participant. A Sonnet conversation that compacted therefore used more than one context window, clearing its cache and building its context anew. Opus 4.7, by contrast, had a one-million-token context window, large enough to hold the entire task from start to finish.

Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions} plots the peak each context window reached across the cohort. A conversation that compacted twice, for example, resolves to three peaks, one for each context window it used. The highest markers are Spec-kit conversations that grew long without a reset. APM's conversations generally sit below them, most in a dense low cluster, since dividing the work leaves each agent with less context to accumulate.

A few APM markers nonetheless reach the high ranges, one even passing the Spec-kit conversation it is paired with. These are all APM Workers, and they accumulate the largest amount of tokens of any APM role, therefore contributing the largest part of its cost, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:cost-session-levels-by-role}.

Under Opus no conversation reset, so each used only one context window, growing from the start of the task to its end. Each call is charged for the whole context before it, and APM's conversations together held more accumulated context than Spec-kit's one. Under Opus, all three paired summaries were positive for output and both cache-write categories. Input was negligible, while the cache-read summaries were positive despite one negative pair. The cache-write differences were positive in every pair, while output and cache-read differences were positive in five of six. Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-model} presents this and the wide gaps of Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-opus} show Spec-kit holding a few lone high markers and APM a spread that reaches toward them.

Under Sonnet, both frameworks faced the tighter window's pressure. Spec-kit's conversation reached the limit and compacted in five of its six sessions, more than once in three. Every APM session had one reset as well; all six facing the context window limits with the Worker assigned to C part of the task, its heaviest work. Four auto-compacted, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-compaction-incidence-by-model} reports, and the other two reset through a Worker Handoff instead, which the table's compaction tally does not capture.

In cost terms a Spec-kit conversation that compacted behaved as a succession of agents, each starting fresh in a new context window, rebuilding cache and accumulating context again, which is why under Sonnet its cost came closer to APM's. This shows in the cache reading. Under Sonnet, Spec-kit read more from the cache than APM, the reverse of the Opus case, as the cache-read row of Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-paired-by-model} reports. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-sonnet} shows why. Spec-kit drove one conversation up to the compaction ceiling and back, paid for a large context on each climb, while APM held most of its conversations well below the ceiling, where each call had to reread less.

A platform issue we observed during the study complicates the Sonnet reading. A model cannot serve a request past its context window, and Claude Code auto-compacts near the limit to prevent reaching it. In four Spec-kit sessions, with every indicator reporting that same model, the conversation passed the auto-compaction ceiling without resetting and went on serving requests past the window. This shows in the highest points of Figure~\ref{fig:disc-occupancy-sessions-sonnet}. These sessions appear to have been served by a larger variant the platform never surfaced. The issue followed the session's Claude Code version, not its framework, but its mark on accumulation shows only in Spec-kit, since no APM session passed the window. That added accumulation plausibly lifted Spec-kit's Sonnet cache reads.

The issue bears little on the cost reading described above. The added accumulation resulted in more cache reading, the cheapest of the token categories. Impacted conversations went only modestly past the auto-compaction ceiling in all but one session, which went about a hundred thousand tokens over. Including this one, three of the four then compacted manually and continued in a new context window; the same behaviour as the Spec-kit and APM sessions that did not meet the issue. The fourth never reset, and accumulated to about fifty thousand tokens over the auto-compaction ceiling. We cannot know how it would have continued had its context been compacted instead. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-threats} treats this as a threat to validity.

\subsection{The Demands on the User}
\label{sec:discussions-demands}

APM keeps the user in control of the build, just as it does of the plan. Its design routes implementation through the user, and the authority over the work stays with them rather than the agent. Spec-kit extends its planning autonomy into implementation, where its agent works through the task list largely on its own. The greater demand APM places on the user is therefore by design, the same authority it provides during planning, while Spec-kit continues with minimal interaction.

That difference appears in the user's input. APM has far more turn prompts than Spec-kit, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-turns-levels} reports, and most are protocol, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition} reports. They are the per-cycle relay APM's design places in the user's hands, each being a dispatch out or a report back rather than an instruction. Standard messages are few under both frameworks, and what little APM has reaches the Manager rather than the Workers, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-composition-by-role} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-process-breakdowns} sets out. APM's added demand is therefore coordination, not deliberation.

Spec-kit asks much less. Its few turns are the protocol command that launches the implementation and an occasional short or standard follow-up, too few to read as a settled composition. The agent proceeds largely on its own, and the user has little to do until the build is done.

Because APM has several agents carry out its implementation where Spec-kit has one, it has a naturally larger model request count. APM issued more requests than Spec-kit in every one of the twelve pairs, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-implementation-behaviour-paired} reports, reversing the planning observation where Spec-kit issued more. Each agent additionally produces its own output, so APM produced more output overall, again reversing planning, as the cost reading of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-mechanics} sets out.

\section{Behind the Functional Score}
\label{sec:discussions-blackbox}

The functional results take the grader's scores at face value. This section looks behind them, first at what a black-box grader can miss and then at what the scores cost.

\subsection{Grading with a Black Box}
\label{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}

The grader judges a submission only by the behaviour of the running program, never its source, as Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures-functional} sets out. A single fault that blocks the program's responses therefore reads as near-total failure, even where the rest of the implementation may be sound.

Three Sonnet sessions show this. Each scored exactly 34 on the total, with the Python half complete and almost the whole C half lost. The cause in all three was the same narrow flaw in the parser their C replica used to read MCP messages. MCP carries requests as JSON-RPC objects\footnote{MCP transport specification available at: \url{https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/basic/transports}}, so the replica had to read each JSON object and decide which operation to perform. Our grader sends requests with ordinary spacing between JSON fields, while an alternative compact form removes those spaces\footnote{For example, \texttt{\{"id": 1\}} uses ordinary spacing, while \texttt{\{"id":1\}} is compact.}. Both forms spell the same object, since the whitespace between fields is insignificant in the JSON the protocol uses.\footnote{RFC 8259, Section 2 (\url{https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8259}).}

A conformant parser should accept both forms. The affected parsers accepted only the compact spelling. Because the first grader request used the spaced form, their C parser was unresponsive and the exchange stopped before the later checks could probe the rest of the implementation. These attempts were otherwise strong. When graded again with the grader's requests being in compact form, the three sessions received diagnostic scores between 93.9 and 100. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-grading-compact-comparison} plots the full Sonnet group, and Table~\ref{tab:disc-grading-compact-sessions} gives the three changed scores.

Five sessions across the cohort scored below fifty. The re-grade recovers three of them, both of APM's and one of Spec-kit's. After it, APM's sessions are all high, and the two that stay below fifty are Spec-kit's. What those two are, this re-grade cannot say. It reaches one kind of grader strictness, and the two it leaves may be sound work it does not reach, just as the three it recovered were, or they may be genuine shortfalls. Recovering two APM sessions also moves the cohort comparison toward APM, but that is one participant rather than a framework effect. The flaw appears under both frameworks, and the participant who shipped it in both sessions barely moves with the re-grade.

This re-grade is therefore a diagnostic, not a fairer score. It reaches one fault class, while the total score stays the basis of the comparison. The grader can hide sound work in other forms this one re-grade does not show, so it bounds, rather than measures, what the scores leave out.

\subsection{Cost versus Score}
\label{sec:discussions-cost-score}

The two frameworks scored level across the cohort, as Section~\ref{sec:results-functional} reports, while APM cost more, as Section~\ref{sec:results-economic} reports. Figure~\ref{fig:disc-cost-score-comparison} sets the two differences against each other. Most participants sit at a slightly positive to no score difference with a positive cost difference. For them APM cost more and the functional outcome was the same. The cost gap is wide under Opus and slight under Sonnet.

The three participants whose APM session scored clearly above their Spec-kit one are the exception. Their APM session cost about the same or a little less, not more. At three sessions this is an observation rather than a result.

  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/discussions/cost_score.pdf}
  \caption{Per-participant paired cost difference and total-score difference, APM minus Spec-kit, marked by model. The reference lines mark no difference on each axis.}
  \label{fig:disc-cost-score-comparison}

What the added cost bought was APM's structure and the user's control over the build, which on this task did not register in the functional score. The task proved to be bounded and self-contained. One agent, with any subagents it dispatched, could carry it largely on its own, so APM's heavier structure had little functional gap to close. Whether that structure earns its cost is beyond what a task of this size can show. Its value would lie in what the study cannot measure here: the control it keeps in the user's hands, the user's sovereignty of the software produced and in work larger or longer than the one tested.

\section{Perceived Versus Measured Behaviour}
\label{sec:discussions-perception}

Section~\ref{sec:results-perception} reports each rating's sign and distribution on its own terms. This section reads them against the measured behaviour underneath, including the value Section~\ref{sec:discussions-cost-score} closed by hedging as beyond the study's harder instruments: the control the user keeps over the build. What follows reads whether self-report validates the measured outcome or diverges from it, and what that divergence exposes about what the instrument set cannot see.

Effectiveness and confidence diverge from the total score outright. Both favour APM by a wide margin, with no participant rating Spec-kit higher on either, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports. Against that, the total score sits on par between the frameworks, with a paired difference of half a point and a confidence interval spanning zero, as Section~\ref{sec:results-functional} reports. The preference is genuine, and it does not track delivered outcome.

What aligns instead is the perception of the process the user took part in. One participant, who used APM in their second session, wrote that they were ``much more confident in APMs output'' despite ``not having manually checked either of the code output'', naming the reason as the framework being ``strict as to enforcing the PRD when speckit was more lenient''. Confidence here reads as trust in a specification the participant helped settle during planning, rather than a judgement of code they verified, echoing the reading Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd} already builds from the score point of view. The Spec-kit side of the same divergence names the absence directly. One other participant wrote that they ``felt lack of control when it came to the deliverables and how they were produced'', and another found it odd that the framework did not operate interactively, letting the agent take its own decisions with the user left out entirely.

Effort is the one item where APM is behind. It was the more effortful framework for a slight majority of participants, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports, but evidently that effort does not track where APM adds turns. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-demands} reads APM's extra implementation turns as the intended user-mediated coordination. Section~\ref{sec:discussions-sdd} reads planning turns the other way. Turn count there sits level between the frameworks, but APM's are the more substantive ones, since its Planner brings real open questions to the user while gathering context. One participant's account matches this directly, writing that ``the most demanding thing was the planning, because there were questions about the whole plan in advance to clarify almost everything about the implementation'', after which ``the whole task was executed by the workers easily without any assistance from me, other than authorizing terminal commands and checking stuff to see if it aligns with the task''.

Steering confirms the same reading. Delivered steers stayed near zero under both frameworks, as Section~\ref{sec:results-process-interaction} reports. Frustration also points the same way. APM was still rated the less frustrating framework, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-paired} reports, despite the added time and turns, so the added burden did not compound into felt friction.

Mental demand and the effort spent checking the AI's output have no measured counterpart at all. That is a deliberate finding. Some of the cognitive work that AI-assisted development demands leaves almost no trace in the instrument set, which is exactly the gap that self-reporting attempts to fill. One participant's account of a Spec-kit session is the clearest instance: ``I didn't have a way to validate the results of the session despite taking the agents word which run tests with the mock instance'' and ``I didn't do my due diligence in reviewing the code output''. That session's total score is the lowest in the cohort, so here the participant's own doubt and the measured outcome point the same way.

\section{Learning Effects}
\label{sec:discussions-learning-effects}

The within-subjects design has each participant carry out the same task twice, with the environment reset between the two sessions so that no code, workspace state, or assistant context passes from the first into the second. Anything a participant took from their first attempt could then lift their second, and left unaccounted it would be indistinguishable from an effect of the framework used. This section reads the cohort for such a carried-over effect, holding to the same reproducible standard as the rest of the chapter and setting transcript reading aside.

That standard fixes what evidence the question can draw on. The chapter measures each session along four dimensions, and the score is not the only one that could in principle bear on learning. How a participant interacted with the framework and its agents, the interaction measures of Section~\ref{sec:results-process-interaction}, might shift as the task grew familiar. The obstacle is that the comparison here is within participant, between two sessions that used different frameworks, and the two demand such different interaction that the difference between a participant's two sessions is already large from the change of framework alone. A learning-driven change would mesh into that framework difference rather than standing apart. The cost a session incurred and the time it took stand the same way, each shaped first by the framework. The score is different in kind, the task's graded outcome on one rubric rather than framework-shaped behaviour, so a within-participant difference is a common measure a practiced participant should raise. We therefore read the score alone to investigate learning effects.

One limit still bounds what the score can show of a learning effect: the rubric's ceiling. Most sessions scored near the hundred-point maximum, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-levels} reports, and nine of the twelve participants already scored above ninety in their first session, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-sessions} details, so a practiced second session had almost no room to register a gain and only three participants entered their second with substantial ground to recover.

Across the cohort the score shows no order effect. Table~\ref{tab:func-score-paired-by-order} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-functional-breakdowns} splits the paired total-score difference by which framework each participant met first, and the two orders' summaries land close together with both confidence intervals crossing zero. A learning effect large enough to bias the framework comparison would separate the two orders, and the split shows none.

Individually, four participants scored higher in their second session than their first, as Table~\ref{tab:disc-learning-by-participant} sets out, though only two by a wide margin. P002 rose 53 points on the total score, and P010 moved little on the score but the widest in the cohort under the re-grade of Section~\ref{sec:discussions-grading-blackbox}, which raises their second session from 34 to 93.9. Both wide gains fell in an APM second session, so for these two the score cannot separate a practice effect from the framework or from how much a single unrepeated session varies on its own. The remaining two gains, of six points and one, are too small to read as signal. Two individual gains do not amount to a cohort effect, and the order split above agrees with this.

Interestingly, two participants gained the other way, toward their first session. P001 and P012 scored 23.4 and 12.3 points higher in their APM session, and for both that session was the first, unpracticed one, so a benefit from practice cannot account for their advantage. This does not exclude the opposite effect, a second session weakened by fatigue or by lower engagement with a repeated task, which would leave the same trace as a framework effect. The order split gives no sign of this either, though it cannot be ruled out for any single pair.

Any learning here therefore stayed with individual participants and left no measured trace in the one outcome that could reproducibly show it. The counterbalanced design keeps such an effect from biasing the framework comparison, spreading it symmetrically across the cohort whichever way it runs, but it cannot confirm or measure the effect on any one participant.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussions-threats}

This section enumerates the internal, external, and construct validity threats and the mitigations applied. Each threat points back to the specific section that motivates it.

\subsection{Internal Validity}

\textbf{Learning effects from the within-subjects design.} The within-subjects design declared in the Case-Study Design (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-case-study-design}) has each participant complete the same task twice, so task familiarity from the first session can influence performance in the second. Counterbalancing the framework order across participants, as described in Sessions and Data (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data}), mitigates the framework-effect confound but does not eliminate the underlying learning curve. The learning-effects examination described in Section~\ref{sec:discussions-learning-effects} reads each participant's two sessions for evidence of carried-over learning and is the primary defence against this threat.

\textbf{Self-reported versus observed measures.} The perception measures described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures} pair the instrumented, transcript-derived measures with self-reported workload, confidence, and perceived framework effectiveness; observed divergences between self-report and instrument are reported as findings rather than absorbed as an unstated limitation. Residual risk remains: self-reports are subject to recall bias and social-desirability pressures that the instrument-side measurements cannot independently correct.

\subsection{External Validity}

\textbf{Generalisation scope.} The case study is conducted on a single AI coding CLI, against a complex software engineering task requiring two-language implementation and legacy-codebase research, described in the Task Specification (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-task-spec}), in a setting positioned between laboratory and field conditions. Findings generalise within this scope and do not generalise to multi-platform settings, dynamic cross-model routing, simpler or less knowledge-intensive tasks, or pure laboratory or natural-setting conditions.

\textbf{Temporal decay of findings.} The AI assistant field is evolving rapidly. Effect sizes and descriptive claims throughout this chapter (rate-card prices, command sets, framework releases, and model availability) are tied to the May 2026 snapshot stated in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-methodology}. The dollar-equivalent cost values reported under Chapter~\ref{chap:results} rest on the rate-card snapshot and are defensible against it, but may require re-estimation under a materially different rate card.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

\textbf{Behavioural input buckets as process-efficiency proxy.} The short, protocol, and standard input buckets defined under the Process measures in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-measures} are extracted from the transcript content for this study. They are not validated against an external coordination-burden instrument. Their validity as proxies for human coordination effort rests on face validity alone: the relative ordering of frameworks along these metrics is robust, but the absolute magnitude of differences is interpretive rather than calibrated.

\textbf{Instrument coverage limits.} The 100-point rubric captures correctness and robustness but not code quality, maintainability, security posture, or long-term technical debt. Metric extraction is further limited to the Claude Code JSONL transcripts and their sibling subagent files that accompany each participant submission, as declared in Sessions and Data (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data}). Any interaction with the AI that took place outside the CLI's transcript surface is invisible to the metric set.

\textbf{Replica versus live Open eClass.} The shared Docker-based replica of the Open eClass platform described in the Task Environment (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-task-env}) pins the platform to a fixed upstream baseline and seeds deterministic fixture data. The shared replica makes the cohort comparable and the grading reproducible, and removes any parity gap between what participants build against and what the evaluation runs against. The trade-off is that the task is performed against a replica rather than against any live university instance of Open eClass. Any property of a live instance that the replica does not reproduce is not exercised by the case study. Such properties include timezone handling of locale-formatted dates, session-cookie behaviour under load, cross-origin interactions with the mock CAS service, and upstream behaviours that have shifted since the baseline commit.
