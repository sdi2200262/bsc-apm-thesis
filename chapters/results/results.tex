\section{Economic}
\label{sec:results-economic}

An APM session cost a median of about thirty-five US dollars under Opus and fifteen under Sonnet, against Spec-kit's twenty-four and fourteen, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-levels} reports. APM was the more expensive of the two under both models, by a wide margin under Opus and only slightly under Sonnet. Within a model each framework's median, Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and mean lay close together, so its own cost spread was tight and near-symmetric. The Opus sessions cost roughly twice the Sonnet ones, as the model's higher rate set the dollar scale, so the two models are read apart throughout. Within each session the two frameworks placed the cost differently across the task's two phases. APM's fell mostly in implementation, at a median of about twenty-seven dollars under Opus and twelve under Sonnet, while its planning cost was the smaller of the two and, unlike implementation, stayed below Spec-kit's.

Split by model, the paired cost difference behaved very differently, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-model} sets out and Figure~\ref{fig:cost-by-model} shows. At six participants the confidence interval is wide and includes zero, reaching from the smallest of those differences to the largest. The Sonnet interval is similarly wide. Those large Opus values pulled the full-cohort mean up, so the cohort-level cost difference comes mostly from the Opus group.

The cost also divides by token category, the kind of token each dollar paid for. As Section~\ref{sec:context-windows-and-context-decay} described, each call reprocesses the whole session so far, and caching lowers the price of the tokens that repeat. Under both frameworks Claude Code cached almost all of the prompt, so uncached input was negligible, a few cents either way. Claude Code writes the one-hour cache for the main agents the user works in and the five-minute cache for the subagents they dispatch. The five-minute writes were negligible too, which leaves the framework's effect on three categories. The one-hour writes reflect the framework comparison most directly, since the framework sets how many main agents the user opens.

On its own each framework's cost fell mostly on cache-read, the largest category under both models, since the repeated prompt is read from cache on every call. Output and the one-hour writes came next, each a few dollars, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-levels} reports. APM's output and one-hour-write costs were above Spec-kit's under both models, while cache-read was the one category to differ in direction between the models, higher for APM under Opus and slightly lower under Sonnet.

Lastly, session order did not reverse the cost difference. APM cost more than Spec-kit whether participants met it first or second, in four of the six who used it first and five of the six who used it second. Table~\ref{tab:cost-order} in Appendix~\ref{app:cost-breakdowns} gives the difference within each order. The central difference was larger and more consistent when participants met APM second, but at six participants in each order the intervals are wide and the two orders cannot be told apart. The cost difference therefore tracks the framework rather than the order of the sessions, consistent with the learning-effects examination of Section~\ref{sec:disc-learning-effects}, which finds no measurable carry-over between a participant's two sessions.

Two sections in Chapter~\ref{chap:discussions} read these differences further. Section~\ref{sec:disc-sdd} traces the planning-phase difference to how the two frameworks carry out spec-driven planning. Section~\ref{sec:disc-cost-mechanics} reads the implementation cost, how working across several agents shapes where the cost falls and why the two model groups diverge.

\section{Process}
\label{sec:results-process}

The process comparison reads how the time each task took and the effort it asked of the user differed between the two frameworks. The session's temporal shape is reported first, its interaction shape second.

\subsection{Temporal Shape}
\label{sec:results-process-time}

An APM session lasted a median of about 119 minutes against Spec-kit's 71, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-duration-fw} reports. APM's durations clustered tightly, with its Hodges-Lehmann estimate and mean close to the median, while Spec-kit's mean and Hodges-Lehmann estimate sat above its median, pulled up by a few long sessions. The two phases divided that time differently. APM's time fell mostly in implementation, at a median of about 77 minutes against Spec-kit's 30, while planning was closer and went the other way, with APM at about 36 minutes against Spec-kit's 49.

The composition held under both models. The one visible difference was in Spec-kit's planning, where the protocol share was lower under Opus than under Sonnet. Table~\ref{tab:proc-buckets-model} of Appendix~\ref{app:process-breakdowns} gives the shares by model.

Within APM the turn prompts also split by agent role, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-roles} reports. The Planner's turns were those of the planning phase, while the Manager and the Workers divided the implementation phase between them, at medians of 6.5 and 5.0 turn prompts. The composition of those turns by role is given in Table~\ref{tab:proc-roles-buckets} of Appendix~\ref{app:process-breakdowns}. The Workers were addressed almost entirely through protocol commands, while the Manager's messages, mostly protocol as well, also included some short and standard text.
