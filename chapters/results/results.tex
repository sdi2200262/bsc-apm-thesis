\section{Economic}
\label{sec:results-economic}

An APM session cost a median of about thirty-five US dollars under Opus and fifteen under Sonnet, against Spec-kit's twenty-four and fourteen, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-session-levels-by-model} reports. APM was the more expensive of the two under both models, by a wide margin under Opus and only slightly under Sonnet. Within a model each framework's median, Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and mean lay close together, so its own cost spread was tight and near-symmetric. The Opus sessions cost roughly twice the Sonnet ones, as the model's higher rate set the dollar scale, so the two models are read apart throughout. Within each session the two frameworks placed the cost differently across the task's two phases. APM's fell mostly in implementation, at a median of about twenty-seven dollars under Opus and twelve under Sonnet, while its planning cost was the smaller of the two and, unlike implementation, stayed below Spec-kit's.

The cost also divides by token category, the kind of token each dollar paid for. As Section~\ref{sec:background-context-windows-and-context-decay} described, each call reprocesses the whole session so far, and caching lowers the price of the tokens that repeat. Under both frameworks Claude Code cached almost all of the prompt, so uncached input was negligible, a few cents either way. Claude Code writes the one-hour cache for the main agents the user works in and the five-minute cache for the subagents they dispatch. The five-minute writes were negligible too, which leaves the framework's effect on three categories. The one-hour writes reflect the framework comparison most directly, since the framework sets how many main agents the user opens.

On its own each framework's cost fell mostly on cache-read, the largest category under both models, since the repeated prompt is read from cache on every call. Output and the one-hour writes came next, each a few dollars, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-composition-by-model} reports. APM's output and one-hour-write costs were above Spec-kit's under both models, while cache-read was the one category to differ in direction between the models, higher for APM under Opus and slightly lower under Sonnet.

Session order did not reverse the cost difference. All three summaries were positive in both order groups. \section{Process}
\label{sec:results-process}

The process comparison reads how the time each task took and the effort it asked of the user differed between the two frameworks. The session's temporal shape is reported first, its interaction shape second.

\subsection{Temporal Shape}
\label{sec:results-process-time}

An APM session lasted a median of about 119 minutes against Spec-kit's 71, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-duration-levels} reports. APM's durations clustered tightly, with its Hodges-Lehmann estimate and mean close to the median, while Spec-kit's mean and Hodges-Lehmann estimate sat above its median, pulled up by a few long sessions. The two phases divided that time differently. APM's time fell mostly in implementation, at a median of about 77 minutes against Spec-kit's 37, while the two spent almost the same time planning, APM at about 36 minutes against Spec-kit's 35.

\section{Functional}
\label{sec:results-functional}

Most sessions scored near the rubric's hundred-point ceiling. APM's twelve sessions had a median total of 95.7 points against Spec-kit's 96.5, as Table~\ref{tab:func-score-levels} reports, so each framework's typical score sat close to the other's and close to full marks. APM's mean of 86.2 and Spec-kit's of 78.8 both fell below their medians, pulled down by a few low sessions rather than by a broad spread. Ten of APM's twelve sessions scored at or above 88 against seven of Spec-kit's, and APM's two below that mark both scored 34. APM's mean and Hodges-Lehmann estimate sat at or above Spec-kit's across the cohort and both model groups. The only exception was the Opus group's Hodges-Lehmann estimate, where the two were within a point. At the cohort level, the medians were close, so the pooled gap between the frameworks lay in the low tail rather than in the typical score. Participants rated APM the more effective framework in eleven of the twelve pairs and the more confidence-inspiring in nine, with no participant rating Spec-kit higher on either item. Under APM the effectiveness ratings clustered at the top of the scale, with every session at four or five and eight of the twelve at the maximum, while the Spec-kit ratings formed two clusters, at two and four, with only two at three. Table~\ref{tab:perc-ratings-distribution} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-perception-breakdowns} sets out the per-framework distribution behind these readings for every rating. APM was the less frustrating framework in eight of the twelve pairs, with its frustration ratings settling at the bottom of the scale and Spec-kit's spread upward across it. Set against this, participants reported investing more effort under APM in seven of the twelve pairs and more under Spec-kit in four.

The remaining items showed no consistent direction between the frameworks. Mental demand leaned towards Spec-kit, with six pairs favouring it, two favouring APM, and four tied. The effort of checking what the AI produced and the effort of instructing and steering it showed no consistent direction. Prior familiarity was low under both frameworks, with the ratings massed at the bottom of the scale for each, so neither framework was the more familiar one at the outset.

A comparative item asked after the second session, grouped by the framework that session used, gave a softer pattern, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-comparative-distribution} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-perception-breakdowns} reports. Among the five whose second framework was APM the verdicts split evenly between easier and harder, with one rating it the same.\footnote{One participant is excluded from this item. Table~\ref{tab:perc-comparative-distribution}'s note explains why.} Among the six whose second framework was Spec-kit four leaned harder and the other two were divided. The second Spec-kit session leaned towards the harder verdict, though neither group was uniform.
