\section{Economic}
\label{sec:results-economic}

An APM session cost a median of about \$35 under Opus and \$15 under Sonnet, against Spec-kit's \$24 and \$14, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-session-levels-by-model} reports. APM was the more expensive of the two under both models, by a wide margin under Opus and only slightly under Sonnet. Within a model each framework's median, Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and mean lay close together, so neither framework's typical session cost rests on a few extremes. The two model groups are, however, read apart, since the Opus sessions cost roughly twice the Sonnet ones, due to the model's higher rate. Within each session the two frameworks placed the cost differently across the task's two phases. APM's fell mostly in implementation, at a median close to \$28 under Opus and \$12 under Sonnet, while its planning cost was the smaller of the two and stayed below Spec-kit's.

Paired within each participant, the APM session incurred the higher dollar-equivalent cost for nine of the twelve, and all three summaries of the difference are positive. The median and the Hodges-Lehmann estimate fall close together and the mean sits somewhat higher, being the mark of a few large values pulling the average up. The Wilcoxon interval excludes zero, so the cohort-level direction holds, though its width leaves the size of the difference imprecise.

The difference behaved very differently in the two model groups. Among the participants who worked with Opus, five of the six APM sessions cost more. The difference was large on all three summaries, with the median and the Hodges-Lehmann estimate close together and the mean a little above. Those differences run from \$3.73 less to \$32.01 more, which at six pairs is the whole observed range and is what the row's wide interval reports. Among the participants who worked with Sonnet, the difference was small on all three summaries and mixed in direction, running from \$2.72 less to \$7.22 more. That range left the two frameworks at about the same cost for that group. Those large Opus values pulled the cohort mean up, so the cohort-level cost difference comes mostly from the Opus group.

Only the implementation phase carries a difference of its own.\footnote{Session activity outside either framework's workflow phases accounts for under one percent of cohort cost, and its interval spans zero, so it is omitted.} APM's implementation cost was the higher in nine of the twelve pairs, with an interval excluding zero, and it set the direction of the overall difference. Its planning cost was the lower in eight of the twelve, but its interval spans zero, so the phase carries no firm difference. The implementation excess concentrated within the Opus group, where APM cost more in all six pairs, against three of the six under Sonnet. Planning stayed mixed in both, at three of the six pairs under Opus and one of the six under Sonnet.

The cost also divides by token category, the kind of token each dollar paid for. As Section~\ref{sec:background-context-windows-and-context-decay} described, each call reprocesses the context accumulated so far, and caching lowers the price of the tokens that repeat. Claude Code cached almost all the context, so uncached input was negligible, a few cents either way. It writes to the one-hour cache for the main agents the user works in and the five-minute cache for the subagents they dispatch. The five-minute writes were negligible too, leaving the framework difference on output, cache-read, and the one-hour writes.

On its own, each framework's cost fell mostly on cache-read, the largest category under both models. Output and the one-hour writes came next, neither reaching \$10 in any cell, as Table~\ref{tab:cost-token-composition-by-model} reports. APM's output and one-hour-write costs were above Spec-kit's under both models, while cache-read was the one category to differ in direction between the models, higher for APM under Opus and slightly lower under Sonnet.

Session order did not reverse the cost difference. \section{Process}
\label{sec:results-process}

The process comparison reads how the time each task took and the recorded interaction differed between the two frameworks.

\subsection{Temporal Shape}
\label{sec:results-process-time}

An APM session lasted a median of about 119 minutes against Spec-kit's 71, as Table~\ref{tab:proc-duration-levels} reports. APM's Hodges-Lehmann estimate and mean sat close to its median, so its sessions fell evenly around it, while Spec-kit's mean and Hodges-Lehmann estimate sat above its median, pulled up by a few long sessions. The two phases divided that time differently. APM's time fell mostly in implementation, at a median of about 77 minutes against Spec-kit's 37, while the two spent almost the same time planning, APM at about 36 minutes against Spec-kit's 35.

APM was the longer session for every participant. Paired within each participant, the difference came to a median of about 36 minutes, close to its Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and its confidence interval excludes zero. The session duration divides into turn time, when at least one agent was working, and inter-turn time. Both were higher under APM in eleven of the twelve pairs.

The two phases did not share that difference equally. The implementation phase was longer under APM in every pair, with most of the extra duration spent in turn time, and it set the direction of the whole-session difference. The planning duration difference was mixed across participants, and its interval spans zero. Its turn time and inter-turn time divided the same way, both mixed in sign, with intervals that include zero, so neither separates the frameworks within the phase.

\section{Functional}
\label{sec:results-functional}

Most sessions scored near the rubric's hundred-point ceiling. The cohort medians were close, at 95.7 points under APM and 96.5 under Spec-kit. Lower scores pulled the mean below the median for both frameworks, but the larger separation among Spec-kit's median, Hodges-Lehmann estimate, and mean shows that they affected its score distribution more strongly.

The model split changes that reading. Under Opus, all three summaries for both frameworks remained near the ceiling. Under Sonnet, APM's median sat well above its Hodges-Lehmann estimate and mean because two sessions scored 34.0 points while the other four scored from 91.0 to 98.0. Spec-kit split into three scores from 29.6 to 40.0 and three from 84.2 to 97.0, placing its median between those clusters and much closer to its other two summaries. Mental demand leaned towards Spec-kit, with six pairs favouring it, two favouring APM, and four tied. APM was the less frustrating framework in eight of the twelve pairs, with its frustration ratings settling at the bottom of the scale and Spec-kit's spread upward across it. Set against this, participants reported investing more effort under APM in seven of the twelve pairs and more under Spec-kit in four. Spec-kit's own effort ratings massed at the bottom of the scale, with half its sessions at the minimum. The effort of checking what the AI produced and the effort of instructing and steering it showed no consistent direction.

The comparison question asked after the second session, grouped by the framework that session used, gave a softer pattern, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-comparative-distribution} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-perception-breakdowns} reports. Among the five whose second framework was APM the verdicts split evenly between easier and harder, with one rating it the same.\footnote{P012 is excluded because their response cannot be assigned to a realised-order group; see footnote~\ref{fn:evaluation-realised-order} in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-sessions-data}.} Among the six whose second framework was Spec-kit four leaned harder and the other two were divided. The second Spec-kit session leaned towards the harder verdict, though neither group was uniform.

Participants also recorded how their session ended against the three-hour cap. Nineteen of the twenty-four sessions finished comfortably within it and five finished near it, nine against three under APM and ten against two under Spec-kit, as Table~\ref{tab:perc-completion-state} of Appendix~\ref{app:results-perception-breakdowns} reports. No participant reported hitting the cap without completing or stopping early.

The order split the other dimensions report on their headline measure has no counterpart here, since a sign count taken separately for each rating item has no single headline to flip.
